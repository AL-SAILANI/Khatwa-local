# STEP grammar and writing analysis: framework and gap list

Source of the framework: a third-party Arabic video course (shared by the project owner; not an official source;
received: a general STEP orientation video, grammar review parts 1 to 3, and a writing-analysis part 4). Only the
framework and routines are summarised, in our own words. Practice questions must be original.

## Orientation facts (third-party, unverified)
- Three sections: reading, grammar and writing analysis (structure), listening. An optional experimental fourth
  section (mixed) may appear and is not scored.
- Attempts: up to 10 attempts within 3 years; about 30 days of waiting before booking again.
- Rough score bands by CEFR level (course estimate, not official): A1 under 40, A2 40-55, B1 55-70, B2 70-85,
  C1 85+, C2 90+. Could inform the predicted-score model later; keep it labelled as an estimate.
- Practice sources: official sample tests, then timed mocks; train skimming and scanning for reading.
- About 29 grammar rules cover most questions; the main skill is detecting which rule a question tests by looking
  at the stem and at how the options differ.

## Core method: detect the rule first
1. Read the sentence with the blank, then compare the options: what differs (form, tense, word class, order)?
2. Name the rule from that difference, then apply its routine below.
3. Eliminate by rule, not by "sounds right".

## Rule routines (grammar parts 1 to 3)
| Area | Routine |
|---|---|
| Articles | a/an for first mention or one of many; the for known, unique, superlative, ordinal; none for plural or uncountable in general. |
| Quantifiers | Check countable vs uncountable first: many/few/a few vs much/little/a little; some/any/a lot of fit both. |
| Prepositions of time and place | at (point, exact time), on (days, dates, surfaces), in (months, years, enclosed areas); since + point, for + duration, by + deadline, in + period ahead. |
| Tenses (12) | Find time words and connectors (already, yet, since, for, ago, yesterday, by the time, while, when, next year); match the tense to them; two clauses must stay logically consistent. |
| Questions | Yes/no questions: auxiliary before subject. Wh- questions: wh-word + auxiliary + subject + verb. Reported questions return to statement order with no auxiliary do. |
| Passive | be + past participle in the tense of the active verb; object of the active sentence becomes the subject; look for "by" or an action done to the subject. |
| Conditionals | Zero, first, second, third and mixed; in the unreal present use were for all subjects; match the if-clause tense with the result clause. |
| Gerund vs infinitive | Decide from the word before the blank (enjoy/avoid/finish + -ing; want/decide/hope + to; preposition + -ing). |
| Modals | Choose by function: ability, permission, obligation, advice, deduction, past regret (should have + p.p.). |
| Relative pronouns | Decide from the first words after the blank: a verb follows then who/which/that; a subject follows then whom/which/that or none; ownership then whose; place then where. |
| Question vs reported question | Direct: auxiliary first. Embedded or reported: normal statement order. |

## Writing analysis (part 4)
- Capitalization: first word of a sentence, the pronoun I, names, days, months, nationalities, languages, titles
  before names; exceptions: seasons, directions as plain words, and common nouns.
- Punctuation: full stop, question mark, comma in lists and after introductory words, apostrophe for possession and
  contractions, no comma between subject and verb.
- Word order: article/determiner, adjective order, adverb position, subject-verb-object.
- Sentence ordering: use cue words (first, then, however, finally, this, such) but logic first: topic sentence,
  support, conclusion; pronouns need their noun before them.

# Question-solving series (parts 1 to 5): what it adds

Source: five more videos from the same third-party course, solving roughly 1000 recalled exam questions (shared by
the project owner; not official). Notes below are in our own words. Coverage: the first ~2000 lines of each
transcript were read closely; the tails were only skimmed, so small rules may still be missing.

## The solving routine the series repeats
1. Read the options first, not the question. What the options are tells the rule: all prepositions, all modals,
   all quantifiers, all pronouns, all verb forms, all word forms of one root, all conjunctions, and so on.
2. Only then read the sentence and test each option by logic, eliminating wrong forms one at a time.
3. When all options are the same verb form, drop the form check and use the time line: find the time clue and
   which action finished first.
4. Many questions are decided by a single word near the blank (a time word, `of`, `than`, `was`, a name).

## Rule routines added or sharpened
| Area | Routine |
|---|---|
| Conjunctions by logic | because = reason; so = result (the clause with the result follows); but/however = unexpected or opposite outcome; and/or = listing; when/while = two events at the same time; for example = an example follows; in addition = new point; in contrast = opposite. Find the cause and the result in the sentence first. |
| Quantifiers | Decide countable vs uncountable from the noun (money, coffee, advice are uncountable). many/few/a few + countable; much/little/a little + uncountable. `a lot` needs `of`; if `of` follows the blank, pick the a-lot/a-large-number form. some = exists, amount unspecified; any = mostly negatives and questions. both takes plural verb (were); neither takes singular verb (was). |
| Capitalization | Compare option by option. Capital only for sentence start, quote start, I, proper names (every word of a multi-word name, titles before names, faculties, months). School subjects are lowercase unless the name of a course or book. A word being first in a list or sounding important does not earn a capital. |
| Relative pronouns | Person before the blank: who (subject) or whom (object) or whose (possession). Thing: which/that. Check the first words after the blank: a verb means who; subject then verb means whom; a noun owned by the antecedent means whose. which is not for people. |
| Tense from connectors | while: continuous (past continuous in the past). before/after/when with a past clause: past simple (past perfect for the earlier one). until/when/by the time in a future sentence: present simple in the time clause, will in the main clause. by the time + past simple: the other clause is past. Time words last year, ago, yesterday = past. usually, every = present simple. |
| Past perfect vs past simple | Past perfect only when one past action clearly finished before another; a single past action is past simple. |
| Present perfect | Life experience or count so far (how many countries have you visited so far), just, already; since + start point, for + length of time up to now. Questions: auxiliary first. |
| since / for / until / by | since a starting date, for a length, until up to a time. Parts of the day: in the morning/afternoon/evening, at night, by day. by + reflexive means alone (by myself). |
| used to vs be used to | used to + base verb: a past habit that stopped. be used to + -ing or noun: accustomed. Decide by whether the habit is past and finished. |
| Passive recognition | The subject receives the action (the box is made, the laptop is repaired). Verb is the third form. Choose the tense from the time clue (three years ago: was written; being repaired now: is being repaired; promise or prediction: will be delivered). Passive word order: receiver, verb, agent with by. |
| Conditionals and wish | Table: zero present/present; first present/will; second past simple/would + base, with were for all subjects (if I were you); third past perfect/would have + third form. wish about now: past simple, were for all subjects (I wish it were Saturday). |
| Gerund vs infinitive | Adjective before the blank: infinitive (easy to spend). ask, promise, decide: to + base. A time word or preposition directly before the blank: -ing. After would like, want: to. |
| Pronoun families | possessive adjective (my) needs a noun after it; possessive pronoun (mine, yours) replaces the noun; reflexive returns to the subject (yourself singular, yourselves plural, himself); each other for mutual action; subject vs object form by the role (the word after the blank decides). |
| Word forms of one root | Adverb describes a verb (worked intensely); adjective describes a noun; suffix -ity/-ness/-ion marks a noun; -ly marks an adverb. |
| Comparison | than = comparative (-er or more); the + superlative (-est or most) for one in a group; adverbs like quickly are not comparatives. |
| Possessive 's | Singular noun: 's (the kid's toy). Regular plural: apostrophe only (the cats' food). parent vs parents (one or two). |
| Titles and punctuation | Mr., Mrs., Ms. take a period. After all, with the meaning "despite everything" takes a comma; with the meaning "after all of them" it takes none. A wh-word followed by a verb is a question (question mark); followed by a noun or subject it is a statement. |
| Question formation | do/does/did + base verb; never "not" before the verb; a wh-word then auxiliary then subject. |
| Word order | Subject, auxiliary, frequency adverb, main verb, object. For paragraph ordering: introduce the person by name first, then pronouns; passive order receiver, verb, agent; the closing sentence uses finally/in the end; logic beats cue words. |
| Articles | A noun the speaker has already identified (the cake I left on the table) takes the; first mention takes a. |

# Question-solving series (parts 6 to 9): what it adds

Source: four more videos from the same third-party course (shared by the project owner; not official). Notes are in
our own words. These four were read in full. The series keeps repeating the "read the options first" routine
above; only new or sharpened rules are listed.

## New or sharpened rules
| Area | Routine |
|---|---|
| Verbs that cannot be passive | Verbs with no object (arrive, happen, go, come) never take a passive form; I arrived at the hospital has no passive. A verb with a real object (deliver the package) can. |
| still / not yet | Something expected but not happened up to now: present perfect (has not arrived yet), not present simple. Present simple is for habits, routines, opinions and facts. |
| Who vs whom | The word right after the blank decides: a verb follows, the person is the doer, use who; a subject then verb follows, the person receives the action, use whom (the manager whom we interviewed). After a preposition: whom (deal with whom). |
| Question word as subject | Who / what + verb with no auxiliary: who took my pen, what happened to him. |
| Adverb of manner in comparisons | Comparing how an action is done needs an adverb (as carefully as); comparing people or things needs an adjective (as careful as). Decide by what is being described. |
| a / an | By sound, not spelling: an before a vowel sound (an official), a before a consonant sound. A singular countable job or role name needs an article (got a job as an official). |
| Purpose | Purpose of an action: to + base verb (or in order to). so that is followed by a subject and a clause, never by to. |
| Quantifier details | quite a few, a small number: countable. quite a bit, a small amount: uncountable. plenty, a lot, a large amount need of. neither goes with nor; both with a plural verb. few = not many, some = unspecified number (use some when no amount is stressed). |
| Possessive answers in dialogue | The reply mirrors the question: Is this your car? answers No, my car is over there, or It is mine. Possessive adjective needs a noun after; possessive pronoun (mine, yours) ends the phrase. |
| enough | After an adjective: old enough, rich enough. Before a noun: enough money. There is no form like to getting; use enough + to + base. |
| Gerund or infinitive after a verb | enjoy, finish, avoid + -ing; want, decide, promise, wish, need + to; make, let, help + base; like and love allow both. |
| Subject-verb agreement of a single task | A gerund phrase or group action counts as one thing and takes a singular verb (collecting insects and worms was the hardest part). |
| Reflexive pronouns | Match the subject: plural subject (Tom and Adam) means themselves; singular you means yourself; plural you means yourselves; enjoy takes a reflexive. There is no theirselves. |
| Modals for guesses | Present guess in progress: may/might + be + -ing (he may be having lunch now). General or future possibility: may/might + base. Past unrealized possibility: could have + third form (he could have gone, but he did not), joined by but, not so. |
| Conditional table (revisited) | Same four-case table as before; check the if-clause first, then confirm the result clause. Third case: past perfect with would have + third form (also negatives: would not have missed). Second: past simple with would + base. |
| Tag questions | Positive statement then negative tag; negative statement then positive tag; the tag repeats the auxiliary and tense of the statement (Mr. Harris has no brothers or sisters, does he?). |
| Time sequence for past perfect | When one past action finished before another, the earlier one is past perfect (she had made the mistake before she noticed). |
| Present perfect continuous and future continuous | Action started in the past and still going: has been studying since 6 a.m. Action in progress at a stated future moment: will be relaxing at this time next week. |
| Passive time choice | Present simple: is made. Present continuous: is being repaired. Present perfect: has been eaten. Past simple with a past date: was founded in 2005. The agent follows by. |
| Punctuation | A comma goes after an introductory clause that has its own subject and verb (After school ended, we travelled to London). Do not put a question mark on a sentence built as a statement. |
| Question vs statement word order | After how/what/where inside a statement, use statement order (I have no idea how they manage), not question order. |
| Frequency adverb and time placement | Subject, frequency adverb, main verb, object, then manner, place, time (I always drink Pepsi late at night). |
| Responding to a suggestion | A suggestion is answered by agreement or refusal, not by thanks or a repeated offer (Sounds good to me). Match the reply to what was just said. |
| Paragraph ordering | Start with the sentence that names the person or topic, not a pronoun; keep the story's chronological order. |

## Gaps this adds
Existing skills cover most of these. New gaps to add or fold in:
- A verb-patterns skill: verbs that cannot be passive, enough, purpose structures (extends gr-passive and gr-gerund-infinitive).
- Tag questions (not in the skill list).
- Modal perfect forms (could have, should have) with unrealized past (extends gr-modals).
- Dialogue-reply questions (what is the best response), which the listening and structure sections both use.
- The "wh-word inside a statement" order rule (noun clauses; extends gr-noun-clauses).

# Key-rules series (videos 1 to 5): what it adds

Source: a second series from the same third-party course, "the most repeated grammar rules in STEP" (shared by the
project owner; not official). Notes are in our own words. Videos covered: prepositions, countable/uncountable
nouns with articles and quantifiers, comparison, pronouns, simple tenses. Cut-off note: transcripts are
auto-captions, so the last minutes of some videos may be missing.

## 1. Prepositions
| Case | Routine |
|---|---|
| Transport | Can you stand inside it (plane, train, bus, bicycle)? Use `on`. Can you not stand inside it (car, taxi, truck)? Use `in`. Moving from A to B using it: `by` (by train, by bus). |
| Time length ladder | The longer the period, the more likely `in`: century, decade, year, season, month, a week (in the 19th century, in the 1950s, in June, in summer, next week). A fixed date or day or a special occasion: `on` (on Monday, on my birthday, on the weekend, on Eid). An exact clock time: `at` (at 10:30, at noon, at midnight). Parts of the day: in the morning/afternoon/evening, but at night. |
| Age | `at` + age (at 12 months). |
| No time given | If a general long-term habit is meant and no date is named, assume the long period (they get up early in the morning). If one particular day is named (Monday morning), use `on`. |
| Start, end, span | since = the starting point (since 2017, since the beginning of the season). by = the deadline or end point (by next Monday, by 2026). for = the length from start up to now (for 12 hours). in = the time remaining from now to finish (in three hours). |
| Place ladder | The larger and less exact the place, the more likely `in`: continent, country, city, district (in Asia, in Saudi Arabia, in New York City, in Al-Hamra). A street or island: `on` (on Ali Street, on an island). A precise address or a specific building, room or office: `at` (at the university, at home, at the office). Positions such as top, bottom, left, right: `at` (at the top, at the left). |
| Test question idea | Every preposition question hides a category question: is it transport, a time point, a duration, a start, an end, or a place? Answer that first. |

## 2. Countable and uncountable nouns, articles, quantifiers
- Countable: can be one, two, three; has plural -s (book, student). Uncountable: no plural; you count it only with a
  measure unit (a glass of water, a kilo of rice, two bottles of milk; the plural goes on the unit, not on the noun).
  Typical uncountables: milk, flour, sugar, salt, water, rice, money, time, enjoyment, information.
- Articles: the = something the sentence already makes specific (the highest score, the bag that sold for the highest
  price, superlatives, ordinals, "the last/most/first" phrases). a / an = unspecified singular countable noun; an
  before a vowel sound, a before a consonant sound. No article = unspecified plural or uncountable noun.
- Process for the question: look at the first word after the blank, decide vowel or consonant sound, decide singular
  or plural, then decide whether the sentence has made it specific. A question that gives the plural -s
  (paintings) removes a and an at once.
- Specific vs unspecific test: "Are they yours?" does not define the paintings; "the paintings the artist drew" does.
- Quantifier map: much + uncountable; many + countable; a lot of / lots of + both; few = not many (countable);
  little = not much (uncountable); a few / a little = some (positive); some = exists, amount unspecified (mostly
  affirmative, countable or uncountable plural); any = zero or none (mostly negative and questions); several +
  countable; a few and several need a plural noun.
- Signal tips: negative sentences more often take any; sentences about a person's number of items ask for many/few
  when the noun is countable.

## 3. Comparison
- Three kinds: comparing two things (than), distinguishing one from a group (the + superlative), equality (as ... as).
- Adjective length: short (one syllable and words ending in -y such as happy, simple) takes -er / -est (smaller than,
  the smallest). Long (three or more syllables, such as beautiful, expensive, difficult) takes more / less ... than
  and the most / the least. Two-syllable words have no single rule: ending -y or -le take the short form; careful
  takes the long form.
- Detection: than in the sentence means two things; the + group word (of all, in the class) means distinguish; as ...
  as means equality.
- Careful traps: -er never goes together with more (not more smaller); the -est form is not used for two things; an
  option with -est and no the is wrong; much can come before a comparative (much harder).
- Irregular: good, better, the best; bad, worse, the worst; far, farther, the farthest; little, less, the least.
  These must be memorized.

## 4. Pronouns (five families)
| Family | Role | Forms (singular then plural) |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Does the action | I, you, he/she/it, we, you, they |
| Object | Receives the action | me, you, him/her/it, us, you, them |
| Possessive adjective | Comes before a noun | my, your, his/her/its, our, your, their |
| Possessive pronoun | Replaces an owned noun; stands alone | mine, yours, his/hers, ours, yours, theirs |
| Reflexive | Subject and object are the same person | myself, yourself, himself/herself/itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves |

Detection routine:
1. Find what is missing: subject, object, a describer before a noun, a whole owned noun, or the same person again.
2. A gap with a verb after it needs a subject pronoun; a gap after a verb or preposition needs an object pronoun.
3. A gap before a noun needs a possessive adjective (the sentence is a noun-phrase sentence with no verb: it is the
   describing slot).
4. A gap that replaces "my book" needs a possessive pronoun; ask whether it is just a person or an owned thing.
5. Gap needing "by oneself" or the same person as the subject needs a reflexive; by + reflexive = alone.
6. In a dialogue, the second speaker mirrors the first: your in the question becomes my in the answer.
7. Plural reflexives match a group subject (the students: themselves); you = yourself (one) or yourselves (many).

## 5. Simple tenses
- English has three time zones, four aspects (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous): 12 tenses. Simple
  tenses are the easiest: one point or one habit, no continuing or finished meaning, no `be` or `have` helper.
- Present simple: the base verb with I/you/we/they/plural; verb + s with he/she/it/singular. It is used for
  habits, regular events, facts and permanent situations, and in the condition part of a future sentence. Signal
  words: always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never, every day, twice a week, five times a day, every winter.
  If the options have no present simple, a past form may rescue a habit-in-the-past question.
- Past simple: verb + ed or an irregular form (understand: understood, buy: bought, make: made). Signal words:
  yesterday, last night, ago, later, a past year or date. The subject does not change the form. Irregular verbs must
  be memorized.
- Past plus past: if two actions happened in the past, check whether the second is continuous (while + continuous).
- Future simple: will + base verb for every subject. Signals: tomorrow, next week, at 11 p.m. tonight, last
  weekend (when it follows a promise), and promises or offers. Do not confuse will with be (no will be + verb
  mix-ups). A sentence like "when school finishes, I will go" has present simple in the when-clause and will in the main
  clause.

## Gaps this adds
- A preposition skill split by category (transport, time ladder, start/end/span, place ladder). The current
  gr-prepositions skill is a single node, so it should become a small chain with category drills.
- A short article-and-quantifier decision chain: sound, count, specific; then quantifier map.
- Comparison by syllable length, plus a memorized irregular table.
- Pronoun families with a "what is missing" first-step drill.
- Simple-tense signal-word cards and an irregular-verb deck (top ~100).

# Key-rules series (videos 6 to 10): what it adds

Source: the same third-party "most repeated rules" series (shared by the project owner; not official). Videos
covered: continuous tenses, verb patterns (infinitive and gerund), third form of the verb, perfect tenses, perfect
continuous tenses. Notes are in our own words. Transcripts are auto-captions, so the ends of a few may be cut off.

## Tense map (the whole 12-tense system)
- Three time zones times four aspects: simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous. The aspect decides the shape,
  the helper verb decides the time.
- Continuous = be + verb-ing. The verb-ing never changes; only be changes with the time: present am/is/are, past
  was/were, future will be.
- Perfect = have + third form. The third form never changes; only have changes: present have/has, past had, future
  will have.
- Perfect continuous = have + been + verb-ing. Only the first helper changes: have/has been, had been, will have been.
- Method: find the helper words in the options first to name the time and aspect, then use the clues below to choose.

## Continuous tenses
| Tense | Clues |
|---|---|
| Present continuous | Happening now (now, at the moment, look, listen: a call for attention to something going on); a temporary habit or temporary situation (this week, these days, I am living in Riyadh for now); a fixed future plan (I am meeting her tomorrow). If will is not offered, a settled plan can use present continuous or be going to. |
| Past continuous | An action in progress at a past moment (when the accident happened I was watching TV); a long action over a past period (all morning, all week, for months); an old situation that no longer holds (we were living in China at that time); the clause after while. |
| Future continuous | An action in progress at a named future moment (this time next week I will be relaxing on the beach). If both will + verb and will be + -ing are offered with a future time marker, pick will be + -ing. |

- when versus while: after when, prefer the simple verb; after while, prefer the continuous verb. When both
  forms are offered, the continuous after while is the more correct choice; if only the simple is offered, take it.
- There is no had been (as a standalone past continuous): *wasn't studying* is right, *was study* is wrong.
- Logic test: if the person is described as ill, the logical present continuous is staying home (is not going
  to school). If the sentence asks "what is X doing?" use continuous.

## Verb patterns: infinitive and gerund
Three forms after a verb or word: infinitive with to (to eat), gerund (eating), bare infinitive (eat).
- An -ing word may be a noun (eating = the act of eating), not a continuous verb; it appears after prepositions,
  as a subject, and after certain verbs.
- Rule 1: choose the gerund when the word is the subject (Graduating is a milestone; Speaking English is
  important). The gerund is the first choice; to + verb is possible but weaker.
- Rule 2: after an adjective, prefer the infinitive (smart to invest; too expensive for me to buy). If to is not
  offered, the gerund can work as a second choice.
- Rule 2b: infinitive of purpose answers why (He went to Riyadh to find a job. Why did he go to China? To study
  Chinese). so alone is never followed by to; so is followed by a subject (so that + clause).
- Rule 3: after a preposition, always use the gerund (interested in becoming; thank you for helping; before leaving;
  after graduating; I made breakfast before going outside).
- Rule 4: after verbs it depends on the verb:
  - Verbs that take the infinitive with to: need, want, seem, would like, plan, decide, hope, promise, agree.
  - Verbs that must have an object before the infinitive (tell, ask, remind, persuade): tell me to study; ask the
    mechanic to fix.
  - Verbs that take the gerund: enjoy, keep, finish, avoid, mind, suggest, consider.
  - Verbs that take the bare verb (no to, no -ing): make, let, and all modals/auxiliaries (can, could, will,
    would, must, should, may, might). I can go, not I can to go or can going.
  - Verbs that accept both with no big change: like, love, hate, begin, start (I like drinking = I like to drink).
    would like is always to (I would like to listen).
  - Verbs that accept both but change meaning: stop, remember, forget. stop eating = quit eating; stop to
    get water = stop in order to do it.
- Practical order when unsure: gerund after a preposition, infinitive after most adjectives and for purpose, bare
  verb after modals, otherwise use the verb list.
- Trap: forms like to getting or to playing do not exist; check that to is followed by a bare verb.

## Third form of the verb (past participle)
- Where it is used: perfect tenses, the passive voice, third conditional (had + third form), past modals (could
  have + third form), and as an adjective after a noun.
- Three shapes:
  1. Regular verbs: verb + ed, the same as the past simple (expect: expected).
  2. Irregular verbs whose third form equals the past simple (let, let, let; buy, bought, bought).
  3. Irregular verbs whose third form differs from the past simple (give, gave, given; drive, drove, driven). These
     must be memorized.
- Check: when the past simple and the third form share a shape, an option like had + past simple form of a
  type-3 verb is wrong, which can also eliminate answers.
- Meaning note: a participle used as an adjective says what happened to the noun (the lost book, the broken table);
  -ing as an adjective describes what the noun does (the sleeping man, the crying child).

## Perfect tenses
| Tense | Form | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| Present perfect | have/has + third form (have with I/you/we/they/plural; has with he/she/it/singular) | Just finished (just, already, yet); recent past with present effect (I haven't seen her this week); life experience up to now (how many countries; I have visited Moscow; three times so far); a duration not yet closed (has known her since he was 10; for three years). hasn't/haven't + yet = present perfect. |
| Past perfect | had + third form for every subject | Two past events: the earlier one is past perfect (when we arrived, the film had already started; after he closed the door, he found he had lost the key). A regret about the past (I wish I hadn't stayed up). Duration up to a past point (by the time he graduated he had spent six years in America). by the time + past simple points to a past perfect in the other clause. |
| Future perfect | will have + third form for every subject | A completion before a future point (by 8 o'clock I will have finished; by this time tomorrow we will have submitted; by then she will have finished; by my next birthday I will have turned 25). Clues: by/by the time/when + a future point. |

Choose-by-clue rules:
- If the option list has have/has with a clearly singular subject like box, pick has (a box of dates is one box).
- Sequence words (after, before, when) tell the order; the verb of the later event is past simple, the earlier is past
  simple or past perfect; if only past perfect is offered, take it.
- Present perfect is not used with a finished past time (yesterday, last year, in 2005) - those take the past simple.

## Perfect continuous tenses
- Present perfect continuous: have/has been + verb-ing. Clues: an action that is still going, with its length
  stated (I have been working for four hours; she has been waiting for half an hour); a cause with a present
  result (I am exhausted because I have been working all day); a recently repeated habit (lately, recently); an
  action since a past point (we have been working together since 2003). A sentence with since or for may allow
  either the present perfect or the present perfect continuous; when a question offers both, look for a stated
  duration, which points to the continuous.
- Past perfect continuous: had been + verb-ing. Clues: a past action that went on and then stopped before another
  past moment (the pavement was wet because it had been raining all day; when I met my friend I had been waiting
  for three hours). The question "how long?" often triggers continuous.
- Future perfect continuous: will have been + verb-ing. Clues: a duration that will reach a stated point (in April
  the teacher will have been teaching here for ten years; by July I will have been living in San Francisco for
  two years; when I retire I will have been working for forty years).
- Cause and result rule: a sustained action in the recent past that explains a present situation is present perfect
  continuous; the same relationship set in the past is past perfect continuous; with a future point it is future
  perfect continuous.
- Reminder: have been is a pair; only have been + -ing is perfect continuous. Do not confuse had been with was.

## Conditionals (if)
- Every conditional has two parts: the condition (the clause with if) and the result. The result may come first and
  the if-clause second (Bob will graduate if he works hard); split by where if starts.
- Memorize the four-case table; the whole question family reduces to it:
  | Case | Meaning | If-clause | Result |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Zero | Always true, 100% (ice melts in the oven) | present simple | present simple |
  | First | Expected, not guaranteed (if you study you will pass) | present simple | will + base verb |
  | Second | Wish, advice, imaginary or impossible now | past simple | would + base verb |
  | Third | Regret, too late | past perfect | would have + third form |
- Method: find the given clause, name its tense, find its row, and fill the other clause from the row. A past
  perfect on one side means the other side must be would have + third form (never plain past simple).
- Choosing the first case or the zero case when the if-clause is present simple: if the result is certain, use
  present simple; if only expected, use will (if you keep the secret, I will tell you what happened).
- were or was: in the second case about something unreal or impossible, use were for every subject (if I were you,
  if I were a bird). Use was only for a real possibility (if I was accepted, I will let you know).
- Past negative result: wouldn't have + third form; a mixture like had + past simple is wrong.

## Clue words and connectors with tenses
- Three ground rules: many questions have no clue word at all; understanding each tense matters more than the
  clue-word list; some clue words fit more than one tense, so check the options.
- Past simple: last night, yesterday, two years ago, a past date or year; irregular verbs need memorizing (they do
  not take -ed).
- when / while: they split the sentence into two events. past simple + when + past simple is typical. After when, the
  simple form fits better; after while, the continuous form fits better. Meaning: when = at that point (when I woke
  up I talked to my mother); while = during (while I was eating, the doorbell rang).
- before / after in the past: the clause right after before is past simple; the other clause can be past simple or
  past perfect. After after, the clause right after it can be past simple or past perfect; the other clause is
  past simple. Do not use these with future-only tense sets.
- Future simple clues: tomorrow, next week, in four days, later (but later can fit other future tenses), dates such as
  in 2032, and promises. will needs a verb (will be/will go), so an option with only will is incomplete.
- when + present simple + future clause: a future-condition pattern (when you arrive, someone will be waiting). Also
  until/as soon as/after + present simple, with will in the other clause. The same pair can have present simple on
  both sides when it describes a habit (when it rains, the road gets slippery).
- Present simple clues: always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, every + time (every Monday, every summer), twice a
  week, weekly, yearly, once a year, seasons (in winter). Watch the subject: no -s with I/you/we/they/plural; -s with
  he/she/it/singular.
- Present continuous clues: now, at the moment, these days, currently, this week; look, listen. Past continuous has no
  reliable clue word, so rely on when/while.
- Future continuous clues: this time next week, at this time tomorrow, later (when it fits a duration).
- A clue only works if the tense form is known: knowing "last year means past" does not help when the right option
  is an irregular verb you cannot recognize.
- An unmarked or broken sentence is wrong: when + past simple + future (when school finished, I will go) is broken
  because the times do not match.
- Tense-with-duration rule: before the other clause, a stated length of time (for hours) points to the continuous
  perfect (when the child arrived, his parents had been waiting for hours).

## Techniques: solve in seconds
Four reusable moves from the technique video; the first two are already in the document but sharpen them:
1. Relative pronouns by sight: find the noun before the blank, decide person or not, then look at the first word or
   two after the blank. Verb first: who (person). Person then verb: whom. Person then person (noun): whose.
   If the options do not include who/whom/whose, use that or which.
2. Verb forms by the word before the blank:
   - preposition before the blank: gerund (about becoming; of flying);
   - before/after/when/while directly before the blank: gerund;
   - an adjective before the blank (important, easy, expensive): infinitive with to;
   - a modal (can, could, must, would): the bare verb;
   - a verb on the infinitive list (seems, need, promise, choose): to + verb;
   - a verb on the gerund list (enjoy, keep, practise, finish): -ing;
   - a subject slot: gerund;
   - a verb that needs an object first (tell, ask, remind): object, then to + verb.
3. Prepositions: in = a month or longer (summer, December, 2019, a season); on = a specific day or a special day
   (on December 19, on my birthday, on the weekend); at = exact clock time (at 9 o'clock, at noon, at midnight, at
   night). A part of the day that is longer than an hour: in the morning/afternoon/evening, but at night.
   Duration vs start/end: for = a length from start to now; in = a length from now to the end; since = start point;
   by = deadline.
4. Tense blanks that sit next to when/while: split the sentence at the connector and match the other clause.
- Note on whether the technique page applies: if the sentence uses a past perfect continuous ("hadn't been"), a
  stated duration of hours or years is the clue.

## Relative pronouns and question words
- A relative pronoun links a described noun to a describing clause. The pronoun must fit the noun.
- Who and whom are for people. Who is the doer (the next word is a verb: the professor who sold...). Whom is the
  receiver (the next words are a subject then a verb: the student whom we met...). When the options offer only who,
  who also covers an object if whom is missing.
- Whose is for ownership: the clause describes something owned by the described noun (the student whose book is
  tidy; the country whose flag is red; the child whose father is famous). The word after whose is a noun, then a verb.
- Which and that are for things (the cake that Salma baked). That also marks a piece of information being passed on
  (the newspaper that he read in). Use which or that when the clause identifies which thing (the shop that is next to
  the pharmacy).
- Adverbs of relation: when = time (the car was wrecked when I drove it); why = reason; where = place where the
  event happened (the hospital where I was born). They differ from pronouns because they refer to an action's time,
  reason or place.
- Question words are the same words used at the start of a sentence: who = which person; whose = whose item; which =
  one chosen from a limited set (which colour did Ahmed choose); what = open choice; when = time; where = place;
  why = reason; how = way or amount (how are you; how much).
- Question versus reported question: a question has a question mark and the question word is first, followed by a
  verb (where are you going?). A reported or embedded question has no question mark, the question word may sit in the
  middle, and a noun or pronoun follows it, then the verb (they asked where my brother went; I could not remember who
  that man was). Do not use an auxiliary after the question word in a reported question; use the base verb after did
  and does only in the real question.
- Detection rules: question mark = question; wh-word then verb = question; wh-word then noun/subject = statement; if
  the option puts a question word at the end, it is wrong.
- Tail trap: if the sentence is a statement, a form with a verb right after the question word is wrong (where was the
  files kept?) and the correct form reads as a statement (where the files were kept).
- so does not belong in relative-pronoun questions: so is a connector of result and the clause with the cause comes
  first (because/when for the cause, so for the result).

## Modal verbs
- Modals (can, could, may, might, must, should, would, will, shall) are followed by the bare verb: no to, no -ing, no
  third form. have to, has to, ought to and had better follow their own forms (has to is not a modal; it follows the
  subject: has to with he/she/it, have to with I/you/we/they).
- Functions:
  - Ability: can, could.
  - Permission/request: can, could, may; may is the most polite for permission; could and would for polite requests.
  - Advice: should, ought to; had better is stronger.
  - Obligation: must, have to; must is stronger. mustn't = forbidden; don't have to = not necessary.
  - Possibility/guess: may, might, could, can; might is the clearest word for "maybe".
  - Offers/promises/the future: will, would.
- Test choice: a statement of necessity (a bleeding person: you must call an ambulance) takes the strongest option;
  a mere guess or assumption (he might want us to pick him up) takes might or may; a polite request takes could or
  would.
- Negative logic: if the sentence needs "you should not drink more coffee", choose shouldn't, not must. If something
  is not necessary (tomorrow is a holiday), choose don't have to.
- Past forms: had to + base verb = past obligation (she had to get ready before every class). must have + third form
  is a guess about the past; should have + third form is a past regret. had must/had to + third form is wrong.
- has did not exist as a modal: forms such as hass, wouldhave, wouldn't be are invalid; check that the verb form
  after the modal is bare.
- Mixed-skill note: some modal answers need the verb after it to be bare too (can eat, must work).
